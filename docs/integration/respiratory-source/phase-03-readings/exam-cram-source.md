# Respiratory System — Exam Cram

*Chapter 23: instructor-aligned recall sheet. Every study-guide question is addressed here, with the vocabulary and identifiable structures emphasized in the two respiratory lectures. Where a slide uses an older term or a simplified explanation, the course wording is retained and the scientific distinction is noted.*

## Seven Functions and Upper/Lower Divisions

The lecture lists **seven respiratory-system functions**:

1. **Gas exchange:** Provides a surface for exchanging oxygen and carbon dioxide.
2. **Air movement:** Moves air to and from the lungs' exchange surfaces.
3. **Environmental protection:** Protects exchange surfaces from dehydration, temperature change, and other environmental variation.
4. **Defense:** Helps prevent pathogens and pollutants from reaching delicate tissue.
5. **Sound production:** Supports speech, singing, and nonverbal vocalization.
6. **Olfaction:** Supplies smell-related sensory information to the nervous system.
7. **Acid–base regulation:** Helps regulate blood **pH** by changing CO₂ elimination.

**Upper respiratory system:** Nose, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and pharynx. It **filters, warms, and humidifies** inhaled air; it can recover some heat and moisture during exhalation.

**Lower respiratory system:** Larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveolar regions of the lungs.

**Conducting zone:** Transports and conditions air from the nose through **terminal bronchioles**. **Respiratory zone:** Begins at respiratory bronchioles and includes alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, and alveoli where gas exchange occurs.

## Nose, Pharynx, and Airflow Route

**Airflow pathway:** External nares (nostrils) → nasal cavity → **choanae** (internal nares) → nasopharynx → oropharynx → laryngopharynx → larynx → trachea → primary bronchi → secondary bronchi → tertiary bronchi → smaller bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveoli.

**Nasal anatomy for identification:**

- **Alae:** Flexible lateral walls of the nostrils; **vestibule:** space immediately inside the nostrils; **vibrissae:** coarse hairs filtering larger debris.
- **Superior, middle, inferior conchae:** Curved projections that increase contact between air and mucosa. **Meatuses:** Passageways beneath the corresponding conchae.
- **Hard palate:** Bony anterior roof of the mouth separating oral and nasal cavities. **Soft palate:** Muscular posterior portion; **uvula:** Posterior projection.
- **Nasopharynx:** Upper pharynx behind the nasal cavity; includes pharyngeal tonsil and openings to the auditory tubes. **Oropharynx:** Behind the oral cavity. **Laryngopharynx:** Inferior shared passage toward larynx and esophagus.

**Benefits of nasal breathing:** Air warming, humidification, filtration, smell, and exposure to locally produced nasal nitric oxide. The slide associates nitric oxide with vasodilation and oxygen uptake; do not interpret this as proof that nasal breathing universally increases blood oxygen saturation.

## Respiratory Mucosa, Lamina Propria, and Mucus Escalator

**Respiratory epithelium:** In much of the conducting airway, a **pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium** containing mucus-producing cells. It traps and transports particles and microorganisms rather than serving as the primary gas-exchange surface.

**Lamina propria:** Underlying loose connective tissue that supports the epithelium and contains vessels, glands, and immune elements where appropriate.

**Mucociliary escalator (“mucus escalator”):** Mucus traps inhaled debris; beating **cilia** move the mucus toward the **pharynx**, where it is swallowed or coughed out. The epithelial lining and lamina propria together form key elements of the airway's respiratory mucosa.

**Quick contrast:** Mucus catches debris; **cilia move it**. Alveolar macrophages handle particles that reach distal gas-exchange regions.

## Regional Histology Identification

- **Nasal cavity (most respiratory region):** Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium; olfactory and vestibular areas are specialized exceptions.
- **Nasopharynx:** Predominantly respiratory-type ciliated epithelium.
- **Oropharynx/laryngopharynx:** Predominantly **nonkeratinized stratified squamous** epithelium, protective against food abrasion. The lecture shorthand “pharynx = stratified squamous” omits nasopharyngeal differences.
- **Trachea and most bronchi:** Pseudostratified ciliated columnar respiratory epithelium with mucous cells.
- **Bronchioles:** Transition toward **simple columnar or cuboidal** epithelium; cilia decrease distally; club cells are important in smaller airways.
- **Alveoli:** Extremely thin **simple squamous epithelium**, largely made of **type I pneumocytes**; minimizes diffusion distance.

**Main principle:** Large airways prioritize conditioning and defense; alveoli prioritize rapid **gas diffusion** across a thin membrane.

## Laryngeal Cartilages, Folds, and Voice

**Larynx functions:** Maintains an open airway, protects the lower respiratory tract, and produces vocal sounds. It contains the **glottis** (air passage between the true vocal folds) and supportive cartilages.

**Three unpaired cartilages:** **Epiglottic, thyroid, cricoid.** **Three paired cartilage types:** **Arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform.** Remember: **3 + 3 categories**, with each paired type represented on both sides.

**Vestibular folds** = **false vocal cords**; help protect the true folds and airway. **Vocal folds** = **true vocal cords**; vibrate as air passes to produce voiced sounds. **Phonation** = generation of vocal sound; **articulation** = shaping it into recognizable speech using tongue, lips, teeth, palate, and related structures.

**Pitch depends on:** Vocal-fold **length, thickness/mass, and tension**. Generally, longer/thicker folds tend to produce lower frequencies; greater tension generally increases pitch.

**Intrinsic laryngeal muscles:** Adjust positions/tension of the folds and open/close the glottis, often by moving the arytenoid cartilages. **Extrinsic muscles:** Position/stabilize or elevate/depress the entire larynx, including movement during swallowing.

**Epiglottis:** Helps direct swallowed material away from the laryngeal inlet. Protection also depends on vocal-fold closure and laryngeal elevation; it is not a stand-alone airtight lid.

## Epiglottis and Cough Reflex Sequence

The instructor's **cough sequence**:

1. An irritant stimulates receptors in the airway (for example, near the laryngeal folds).
2. Sensory signals reach reflex pathways in the **medulla**.
3. The glottis briefly closes while expiratory/chest and abdominal muscles compress the lungs, raising intrathoracic pressure.
4. The glottis suddenly opens.
5. Rapidly expelled air helps dislodge and remove material.

**Do not confuse:** **Epiglottis** assists swallowing protection; the **glottis** is the opening between vocal folds; **coughing** uses glottic closure and an abrupt release of air.

## Trachea, Carina, Bronchi and Autonomic Effects

**Trachea:** Reinforced by approximately **15–20 C-shaped cartilage supports** in the anatomy lecture. The open posterior side accommodates esophageal expansion. **Trachealis smooth muscle** bridges the posterior ends of the rings and can adjust airway diameter. **Elastic connective tissue** helps allow length changes and recoil.

**Autonomic effect (lecture association):** Sympathetic input generally favors trachealis relaxation and greater airway diameter; parasympathetic input favors contraction and narrowing. Bronchial and bronchiolar responses also depend on local signals and pharmacology; do not assume identical innervation of every airway segment.

**Carina:** Internal ridge at the tracheal bifurcation into the primary bronchi.

**Branching sequence:** Primary (main) bronchi → secondary (lobar) bronchi → tertiary (segmental) bronchi → smaller bronchi → **bronchioles** → terminal bronchioles → respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveoli.

**Right main bronchus:** Usually **wider and more vertical** than left. **Secondary bronchi:** **3 on the right, 2 on the left**, matching lung lobes.

**Bronchi have cartilage; bronchioles lack cartilage**, relying more on smooth muscle for changing diameter. **Sympathetic/β₂ signaling:** Generally bronchodilation. **Parasympathetic/cholinergic signaling:** Generally bronchoconstriction.

## Lung Lobes, Fissures, and Pleura

**Right lung:** **Three lobes**—superior, middle, inferior; **horizontal fissure** between superior and middle, and **oblique fissure** separating the inferior lobe from the others.

**Left lung:** **Two lobes**—superior and inferior; one **oblique fissure**; **cardiac notch** makes room for part of the heart.

**Apex:** Superior tip. **Base/diaphragmatic surface:** Inferior surface resting against the diaphragm. **Costal surface:** Faces ribs. **Mediastinal surface:** Faces mediastinum. **Hilum:** Entry/exit region for bronchi, pulmonary vessels, nerves, and lymphatics.

**Visceral pleura:** Serous membrane directly covering the lung. **Parietal pleura:** Lines the inner thoracic cavity. **Pleural cavity:** Thin fluid-containing potential space between the layers; helps transmit movement while reducing friction.

**Model-identification priorities:** Larynx, trachea, carina, primary/secondary/tertiary bronchi, right/left lung, lobes, fissures, cardiac notch, apex, base, pleural layers, and diaphragm.

## Alveolar Cell Types and Respiratory Membrane

**Three important alveolar cell types from the lecture:**

1. **Type I pneumocytes:** Thin **simple squamous epithelial cells**, making up most of the gas-exchange surface; O₂ and CO₂ diffuse across them.
2. **Type II pneumocytes (septal cells):** Secrete **pulmonary surfactant**, which reduces surface tension and helps prevent alveolar collapse.
3. **Alveolar macrophages (“dust cells”):** Phagocytose inhaled particles and microorganisms.

**Alveolar ducts** carry air from respiratory bronchioles toward alveolar sacs/alveoli. Alveoli are wrapped in a rich **pulmonary capillary network**.

**Respiratory membrane:** Primarily **type I alveolar epithelium + closely apposed/fused basement membranes + capillary endothelium** (with a thin fluid/surfactant film on the air side). **Gas diffusion occurs here.** Its extremely small thickness reduces diffusion distance.

**Surfactant** is a lipid/protein mixture, not simply “oil.” It reduces the force needed to inflate the lungs.

## External, Internal, and Cellular Respiration

The instructor organizes **external respiration into three steps**:

1. **Pulmonary ventilation:** Air moves into and out of the alveoli.
2. **Pulmonary gas exchange (diffusion):** O₂ crosses into blood; CO₂ crosses into alveolar air.
3. **Transport in blood:** Circulating blood carries O₂ and CO₂ between lungs and tissues.

**Internal respiration:** Gas exchange between systemic capillary blood and body tissues: tissues take up O₂ and release CO₂.

**Cellular respiration:** Metabolic pathways in cells that use chemical energy from fuels to make ATP; aerobic metabolism uses O₂ as the final electron acceptor.

**Terminology note:** Many physiology texts use *external respiration* more narrowly for gas exchange at the lungs, while the lecture groups ventilation, lung exchange, and blood transport together. Learn the instructor's **three-step list** for the exam.

## Boyle’s Law and Pressure Direction

**Boyle's law:** For a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature, **P × V = constant**, or **P₁V₁ = P₂V₂**. Increasing volume lowers pressure; decreasing volume raises pressure.

**Ventilation follows pressure gradients:** Air moves from higher to lower pressure. During inspiration, expansion of the chest/lungs makes alveolar pressure slightly **less than atmospheric**. During expiration, elastic recoil makes alveolar pressure slightly **greater than atmospheric**.

**Illustrative lecture numbers:** Atmospheric pressure **760 mmHg**; inspiring alveoli roughly **759 mmHg**; expiring alveoli roughly **761 mmHg**. Actual pressures vary with breathing conditions and altitude.

**Intrapulmonary (alveolar) pressure** = pressure of gas inside alveoli. **Intrapleural pressure** = pressure in pleural space; these are not the same value. The lungs follow chest-wall movements because of pleural mechanics and the pressure relationship across the lung wall.

**Fast recall:** **Volume ↑ → pressure ↓ → air enters. Volume ↓ → pressure ↑ → air leaves.**

## Inspiration, Expiration, Primary Muscles

**Quiet breathing (eupnea):** Inspiration is **active**; expiration is generally **passive** due to elastic recoil.

**Inspiration:** Diaphragm contracts and descends; external intercostals elevate the ribs; **thoracic volume increases**; **alveolar pressure decreases** relative to atmosphere; air enters.

**Expiration:** Diaphragm relaxes and rises; rib cage returns; thoracic/lung volume **decreases**; alveolar pressure increases; air leaves.

**Two primary inspiratory muscles:** **Diaphragm** and **external intercostals**. Know both from the slides.

**Forced breathing (lecture's “hyperpnea”):** Inspiration and expiration involve greater muscular work. Accessory inspiratory muscles can include **sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, pectoralis minor, serratus anterior**. Forced expiration recruits **abdominal muscles and internal intercostals** (and other relevant muscles).

**Important contrast:** Forced expiration is actively generated; quiet expiration normally does **not** require an active push from abdominal muscles.

## Four Volumes, Vital Capacity, and Total Lung Capacity

Know these **four basic lung volumes**:

- **Tidal volume (TV):** Air moved during a normal quiet breath (illustratively around **500 mL** in the lecture's standard example).
- **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV):** Extra air inhaled beyond an ordinary tidal inspiration.
- **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV):** Extra air forcibly exhaled beyond an ordinary tidal expiration.
- **Residual volume (RV):** Air still present after maximal forced expiration; cannot be measured by ordinary simple spirometry alone.

**Major capacities:**

- **Inspiratory capacity (IC) = TV + IRV.**
- **Functional residual capacity (FRC) = ERV + RV.**
- **Vital capacity (VC) = IRV + TV + ERV.**
- **Total lung capacity (TLC) = IRV + TV + ERV + RV = VC + RV.**

**Key distinction:** Vital capacity excludes residual volume; total lung capacity includes it. Capacity is a **sum of component volumes**. Sample numerical values in the lecture plot are illustrative and differ with body size, sex, age, and health.

## Henry’s Law, Gas Gradients, and Exchange

**Partial pressure:** The contribution made by one gas to the total pressure of a gas mixture, often written **Pₒ₂** or **P꜀ₒ₂**.

**Henry's law:** At a given temperature, the concentration of a gas dissolved in a liquid is related to its **partial pressure** and solubility. A higher partial pressure generally drives more of that gas into solution.

**Diffusion direction:** Gases move down their **own partial-pressure gradients**, not according to the total pressure of the blood or atmosphere.

- **At lungs:** Alveolar Pₒ₂ is higher than in arriving venous blood, so **O₂ diffuses into blood**. Arriving venous blood generally has higher P꜀ₒ₂ than alveolar air, so **CO₂ diffuses into alveoli**.
- **At tissues:** Systemic arterial blood has higher Pₒ₂ than metabolizing tissues, so **O₂ diffuses into tissues**. Producing cells have higher P꜀ₒ₂ than incoming blood, so **CO₂ diffuses into blood**.

**Reason this works:** The respiratory membrane is thin, and both O₂ and CO₂ are able to diffuse across it. CO₂ is much more soluble than O₂, which helps explain its effective diffusion despite smaller usual partial-pressure differences.

## Oxyhemoglobin, Carbaminohemoglobin, and Saturation

**Hemoglobin (Hb):** The major oxygen transporter inside red blood cells.

- **Oxyhemoglobin (HbO₂):** Hemoglobin reversibly bound to **oxygen**.
- **Carbaminohemoglobin (HbCO₂):** Hemoglobin with **CO₂ bound to globin sites**; this is a distinct form from oxygen binding to heme.
- **Pₒ₂ rises:** Tends to favor oxygen loading. **Pₒ₂ falls:** Tends to favor oxygen unloading.
- **Lower pH / more CO₂ in tissues:** Generally favors oxygen release (the **Bohr effect**).

**Oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve:** **S-shaped**, due to cooperative binding. At high Pₒ₂ (lung conditions), hemoglobin saturation is high and the curve is flatter. At lower Pₒ₂ (working tissue conditions), the slope is steeper, supporting substantial oxygen unloading.

**Right shift** (e.g., increased CO₂, H⁺, temperature): Lower oxygen affinity at a given Pₒ₂, favoring unloading. **Left shift** generally reflects higher affinity.

**Color correction:** Oxygenated blood is typically bright red and deoxygenated venous blood darker red. It is **not blue**, despite common schematic conventions.

## Three CO2 Routes, Carbonic Anhydrase, and pH

The lecture gives the approximate CO₂ transport breakdown **70% / 23% / 7%**:

1. **~70%:** Transport through the **bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) pathway**.
2. **~23%:** Bound to hemoglobin and other proteins (including **carbaminohemoglobin**).
3. **~7%:** **Physically dissolved** in blood plasma.

The slides call the ~70% fraction “carbonic acid.” For chemical accuracy, **carbonic acid is a transient intermediate**, whereas bicarbonate is the dominant transported form.

**Carbonic anhydrase** in red blood cells catalyzes the hydration/dehydration of CO₂:

**CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻**

**Chloride shift:** HCO₃⁻ often leaves an RBC in exchange for Cl⁻ during systemic CO₂ pickup; this exchange reverses in pulmonary capillaries. Hemoglobin and other blood buffers help bind H⁺.

**Connection to pH:** More retained CO₂ tends to push the equilibrium toward more H⁺ and **lower pH** (respiratory acidosis if sufficiently severe). Increased elimination of CO₂ tends to reduce H⁺ and **raise pH** (respiratory alkalosis if sufficiently severe). Lungs regulate CO₂ rapidly; kidneys provide additional longer-term acid–base control.

## Medullary DRG/VRG and Pontine Centers

**Respiratory rhythmicity centers:** Medullary networks that help generate/coordinate rhythmic breathing. In the lecture's scheme they are located in the **medulla oblongata**.

**Dorsal respiratory group (DRG):** Associated with basic inspiratory activity and sensory integration; the lecture emphasizes signals to the **diaphragm and external intercostals** during quiet and forced breathing.

**Ventral respiratory group (VRG):** Participates in more extensive respiratory motor patterns and stronger respiratory effort; the lecture emphasizes recruitment of **accessory respiratory muscles**, especially during **forced breathing**.

**Pontine centers (older teaching terminology):**

- **Apneustic center:** Described in the lecture as promoting prolonged/strong **inspiratory drive** and stimulating the DRG.
- **Pneumotaxic center:** Described as limiting inspiratory drive, contributing to the switch toward expiration, and inhibiting apneustic influence.

**Locations:** Both pontine terms refer to functional influences from the **pons**. The study-guide question “Which stimulates the DRG?” expects **apneustic**.

**Scientific nuance:** Modern respiratory physiology describes interacting medullary and pontine neuronal networks, including rhythm-generating circuitry not captured by these older discrete “center” labels. Know the instructor's names without assuming they are isolated anatomical switches.

**Chemoreceptors:** Central mechanisms respond strongly to **CO₂-dependent pH changes** near the medulla; peripheral carotid and aortic chemoreceptors respond to changes in blood oxygen, CO₂, and pH. **Stretch and irritant receptors** also influence reflex breathing.

## Named Conditions and Rapid Anatomy Recall

Lecture conditions:

- **Hypoxia:** Inadequate oxygen availability at the tissue level.
- **Anoxia:** Near-complete or complete lack of available oxygen to tissue; the slide uses “apoxia,” a less common term for absent oxygen supply.
- **Laryngitis:** Inflammation of the larynx, commonly producing hoarseness from changed vocal-fold vibration.
- **Acute epiglottitis:** Inflammation/swelling of the epiglottic region that can threaten airway patency; potentially urgent.
- **Pneumonia:** Infection/inflammation of lung parenchyma, often involving alveoli and impaired gas exchange.
- **Cystic fibrosis:** Genetic disease disrupting epithelial ion transport, leading to dehydrated, viscous airway secretions and impaired clearance.
- **Asthma:** Variable airway narrowing with inflammation and often bronchial hyperresponsiveness; not every episode is simply an allergic reaction.

**Quick anatomy identification checklist:** Superior/middle/inferior nasal conchae and meatuses; hard/soft palate; uvula; naso-, oro-, laryngopharynx; epiglottis; thyroid, cricoid, arytenoid, corniculate and cuneiform cartilages; vocal/vestibular folds; glottis; tracheal cartilages; trachealis; carina; primary/secondary/tertiary bronchi; right and left lung lobes/fissures; left cardiac notch; apex/base; pleura; diaphragm; alveolar ducts and alveoli.

**Last-minute associations:** Type I = thin diffusion cell. Type II = surfactant. Macrophage = defense. Sympathetic = bronchodilation. Parasympathetic = bronchoconstriction. Boyle = volume versus pressure. Henry = gas dissolved in liquid. O₂ loads at lung; unloads at working tissue. Most CO₂ = bicarbonate pathway. DRG = basic inspiratory circuitry. VRG = stronger/forced respiratory output. Apneustic = promotes inspiration; pneumotaxic = limits inspiration (lecture wording).
