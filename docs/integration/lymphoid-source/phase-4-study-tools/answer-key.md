## Multiple Choice

### mc-001 · fluid
What are the three principal functions of the lymphatic system emphasized in Chapter 22?
**Answer:** Return tissue fluid to blood, support immune defense, and transport absorbed intestinal fats
**Explanation:** The anatomy lecture names fluid recovery, immune response, and fat absorption as the three major functions.

### mc-002 · fluid
A patient develops swelling after lymph vessels are surgically damaged. Which mechanism best explains the swelling?
**Answer:** Interstitial fluid is not being returned to the blood efficiently
**Explanation:** Blocked lymphatic return can accumulate fluid in tissues, producing lymphedema.

### mc-003 · fluid
A fluid sample is taken from between muscle cells and then from an adjacent lymphatic vessel. What changes in its name?
**Answer:** It is called interstitial fluid in tissue and lymph inside the lymphatic vessel
**Explanation:** The names describe the fluid's compartment; entry to a lymphatic vessel makes it lymph.

### mc-004 · fluid
Why is the lymphatic system important to maintaining blood volume?
**Answer:** It returns part of the fluid filtered out of blood capillaries to the venous circulation
**Explanation:** Lymphatic fluid recovery supports normal circulation by returning interstitial fluid.

### mc-005 · fluid
Which situation would most directly increase interstitial edema despite normal heart pumping?
**Answer:** Obstruction of local collecting lymphatics
**Explanation:** Edema can arise when local fluid entry exceeds lymphatic removal.

### mc-006 · fluid
After a meal rich in long-chain triglycerides, which fluid may have a milky appearance?
**Answer:** Chyle in intestinal lymphatics
**Explanation:** Chyle is lymph enriched in chylomicrons, which can make it appear milky.

### mc-007 · fluid
Which pressure generally promotes movement of water outward across blood capillaries?
**Answer:** Capillary hydrostatic pressure
**Explanation:** Hydrostatic pressure helps drive filtration toward surrounding tissues.

### mc-008 · fluid
Which statement best connects lymphatic and circulatory anatomy?
**Answer:** Lymph ultimately returns to venous blood near the junctions of jugular and subclavian veins
**Explanation:** Lymphatic drainage completes a fluid-return loop into venous circulation.

### mc-009 · capillaries
Which structural difference helps initial lymphatic capillaries take up interstitial fluid?
**Answer:** Their overlapping endothelial cells form pressure-sensitive entry flaps
**Explanation:** Overlapping endothelial edges favor inward entry from tissue.

### mc-010 · capillaries
Why are initial lymphatic capillaries described as blind-ended?
**Answer:** They begin as closed-end pockets in tissues rather than forming blood-capillary loops
**Explanation:** Initial lymphatics start as blind-ended uptake structures.

### mc-011 · capillaries
What happens when tissue pressure rises outside a lymphatic capillary?
**Answer:** Entry flaps can open to admit fluid and anchoring filaments can help prevent collapse
**Explanation:** Tissue pressure can favor inward uptake; anchoring filaments support capillary patency.

### mc-012 · capillaries
Why do collecting lymphatic vessels contain many valves?
**Answer:** Their low-pressure flow requires protection against backflow
**Explanation:** Valves maintain net one-way transport despite intermittent compression and low pressure.

### mc-013 · capillaries
A person walks after sitting immobile for hours. Why might lymph return from the legs improve?
**Answer:** Muscle contractions compress collecting vessels and valves direct the fluid centrally
**Explanation:** The skeletal-muscle pump assists movement past one-way valves.

### mc-014 · capillaries
How does breathing support lymph drainage?
**Answer:** Changes in thoracic and abdominal pressure help draw and propel lymph centrally
**Explanation:** Pressure gradients generated by respiration supplement collecting-vessel contraction.

### mc-015 · capillaries
Where would chylomicrons first enter the lymphatic network after leaving intestinal epithelial cells?
**Answer:** Lacteals within small-intestinal villi
**Explanation:** Lacteals specialize in receiving intestinal lipid particles.

### mc-016 · capillaries
Which statement about lymphatic pumping is most accurate?
**Answer:** Flow uses valves, body movement, breathing, and rhythmic collecting-vessel contraction without a single heart-like pump
**Explanation:** Multiple distributed mechanisms drive low-pressure lymph return.

### mc-017 · drainage
Which territory usually drains toward the right lymphatic duct?
**Answer:** Right head and neck, right upper limb, and right thorax
**Explanation:** The right upper quadrant is the usual right lymphatic duct territory.

### mc-018 · drainage
Which duct returns lymph from most of the body?
**Answer:** Thoracic duct
**Explanation:** The thoracic duct drains both lower quadrants and the left upper quadrant.

### mc-019 · drainage
Which three named trunks are the key inputs for the right lymphatic duct in the lecture?
**Answer:** Right jugular, right subclavian, right bronchomediastinal
**Explanation:** The right upper quadrant uses the right-sided jugular, subclavian, and bronchomediastinal trunks.

### mc-020 · drainage
Which three trunks commonly converge near the cisterna chyli in the class diagram?
**Answer:** Right lumbar, left lumbar, and intestinal trunks
**Explanation:** Lumbar and intestinal trunks collect inferior-body and intestinal lymph.

### mc-021 · drainage
A lymphatic tracer enters the LEFT foot. Which route is appropriate?
**Answer:** Left lumbar drainage toward cisterna chyli, thoracic duct, then left venous angle
**Explanation:** The thoracic duct returns lymph from both lower limbs.

### mc-022 · drainage
Where does the thoracic duct usually terminate?
**Answer:** Near the junction of the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins
**Explanation:** The usual terminal site is the left venous angle.

### mc-023 · drainage
Which structure is an expanded chamber near the inferior start of the thoracic duct?
**Answer:** Cisterna chyli
**Explanation:** The cisterna chyli receives lymph from lumbar and intestinal territories.

### mc-024 · drainage
Which trunk normally collects lymph from an upper limb?
**Answer:** Subclavian trunk on that side
**Explanation:** The right and left subclavian trunks drain their respective upper limbs.

### mc-025 · nodes
Which lymphatic vessels convey lymph INTO a lymph node?
**Answer:** Afferent lymphatic vessels
**Explanation:** Afferent means arriving at a node; efferent means leaving.

### mc-026 · nodes
Where does lymph first arrive inside a typical node after entering via afferent vessels?
**Answer:** Subcapsular sinus
**Explanation:** The subcapsular sinus receives incoming lymph beneath the capsule.

### mc-027 · nodes
Which region of a lymph node is particularly enriched in B-cell follicles?
**Answer:** Outer cortex
**Explanation:** Follicles are characteristic of the outer cortex.

### mc-028 · nodes
Where are many T lymphocytes concentrated in a lymph node?
**Answer:** Paracortex (deep cortex)
**Explanation:** The paracortex is a T-cell-rich zone.

### mc-029 · nodes
What is the principal activity of a germinal center during a response?
**Answer:** Activated B-cell proliferation and affinity-related selection
**Explanation:** Germinal centers support B-cell proliferation and maturation after activation.

### mc-030 · nodes
Which sequence is closest to the physical pathway of LYMPH through a node?
**Answer:** Afferent vessel → subcapsular sinus → cortical/medullary sinuses → efferent vessel
**Explanation:** Fluid travels through sinus spaces; the B-cell and T-cell regions are nearby immune compartments.

### mc-031 · nodes
What is the functional advantage of bringing antigen and lymphocytes into lymph nodes?
**Answer:** It facilitates antigen sampling and encounters among antigen-presenting cells and lymphocytes
**Explanation:** Nodes both filter lymph and coordinate adaptive activation.

### mc-032 · nodes
How can circulating naive lymphocytes enter a lymph node without arriving in afferent lymph?
**Answer:** Through specialized high endothelial venules
**Explanation:** High endothelial venules allow lymphocyte entry from the blood.

### mc-033 · lymphocytes
Where do human B lymphocytes normally develop and mature?
**Answer:** Bone marrow
**Explanation:** B cells are bone-marrow-derived; their later activation may occur in secondary lymphoid tissues.

### mc-034 · lymphocytes
Where do T lymphocytes mature and undergo selection?
**Answer:** Thymus
**Explanation:** T-cell precursors originate from marrow but mature in the thymus.

### mc-035 · lymphocytes
Which lymphocyte class belongs primarily to innate defense?
**Answer:** Natural killer cells
**Explanation:** NK cells act through innate surveillance and killing.

### mc-036 · lymphocytes
Which pair are the principal antigen-specific adaptive lymphocyte classes?
**Answer:** B cells and T cells
**Explanation:** B and T lymphocytes use clonally generated antigen receptors and can form memory.

### mc-037 · lymphocytes
What is the central purpose of the blood–thymus barrier, particularly in the cortex?
**Answer:** Limit premature exposure of developing thymocytes to circulating antigens
**Explanation:** The barrier supports the developing thymocyte environment.

### mc-038 · lymphocytes
Why does thymic negative selection matter?
**Answer:** It helps remove or control developing T cells that strongly recognize self-antigens
**Explanation:** Strong self-reactivity is a danger for autoimmune responses.

### mc-039 · lymphocytes
Which process favors survival of thymocytes capable of recognizing self-MHC sufficiently?
**Answer:** Positive selection
**Explanation:** Positive selection establishes functional MHC recognition; negative selection addresses excessive self-reactivity.

### mc-040 · lymphocytes
What is thymic involution?
**Answer:** Age-related reduction of thymic lymphoid tissue with replacement by fatty/connective tissue
**Explanation:** Involution reduces thymic volume over time but does not imply that all immune defenses disappear.

### mc-041 · spleen
How does splenic immune surveillance differ fundamentally from lymph-node surveillance?
**Answer:** Spleen primarily filters blood, whereas lymph nodes primarily filter lymph
**Explanation:** The spleen responds to blood-borne antigens and removes abnormal blood cells.

### mc-042 · spleen
Which splenic compartment is enriched in lymphocytes and immune responses to blood antigens?
**Answer:** White pulp
**Explanation:** White pulp is lymphocyte-rich.

### mc-043 · spleen
Which splenic compartment is associated with removal of damaged or senescent red blood cells?
**Answer:** Red pulp
**Explanation:** Red pulp is blood-rich and contains macrophages that clear old or abnormal RBCs.

### mc-044 · spleen
What enters and leaves the spleen at its hilum?
**Answer:** Splenic artery and vein
**Explanation:** The hilum is the major vascular gateway of the spleen.

### mc-045 · spleen
What happens to iron from many aged red blood cells removed by splenic macrophages?
**Answer:** It can be recovered and recycled for future hemoglobin synthesis
**Explanation:** Macrophages help recover iron from hemoglobin.

### mc-046 · spleen
Which surface of the spleen conforms to the diaphragm?
**Answer:** Diaphragmatic surface
**Explanation:** The smooth outer diaphragmatic surface is a gross anatomical landmark.

### mc-047 · spleen
Which consequence is clinically important after removal of the spleen?
**Answer:** Increased susceptibility to severe infections by some encapsulated bacteria
**Explanation:** Splenic clearance of certain blood-borne encapsulated microbes is especially valuable.

### mc-048 · spleen
Why can trauma to the spleen be medically dangerous?
**Answer:** Its highly vascular tissue can bleed extensively into the abdomen
**Explanation:** Splenic injury can cause major internal hemorrhage and shock.

### mc-049 · malt
Which characteristic distinguishes the three encapsulated lymphoid organs named in the instructor's comparison?
**Answer:** They have a recognizable connective-tissue capsule
**Explanation:** The course lists nodes, thymus, and spleen as encapsulated organs.

### mc-050 · malt
Which pair is usually classified as PRIMARY lymphoid sites for lymphocyte development?

**Explanation:** The distinction concerns lymphocyte maturation, not merely the presence of a capsule.

### mc-051 · malt
What is the principal location of Peyer's patches?
**Answer:** Small intestine, especially the ileum
**Explanation:** Peyer's patches are aggregated intestinal lymphoid follicles.

### mc-052 · malt
Where is the pharyngeal tonsil located?
**Answer:** Roof/posterior wall of the nasopharynx
**Explanation:** The pharyngeal tonsil is the adenoid region.

### mc-053 · malt
Which tissue grouping helps protect mucosal surfaces such as gut and airways?
**Answer:** Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT)
**Explanation:** MALT positions immune cells near surfaces exposed to external material.

### mc-054 · malt
What does GALT stand for?
**Answer:** Gut-associated lymphoid tissue
**Explanation:** GALT is the intestinal/mucosal component of the lymphoid system.

### mc-055 · malt
Which collection of tissue surrounds the opening of the pharynx?
**Answer:** Waldeyer's ring of tonsillar lymphoid tissue
**Explanation:** Waldeyer's ring includes palatine, pharyngeal, and lingual tonsillar tissues.

### mc-056 · malt
What is the immunological role of specialized intestinal M cells?
**Answer:** Transport antigens from the intestinal lumen toward underlying mucosal immune tissue
**Explanation:** M cells help sample luminal antigen near intestinal immune follicles.

### mc-057 · innate
Which is the instructor's exact group of seven nonspecific defense categories?
**Answer:** Physical barriers, phagocytic cells, immunological surveillance, interferons, complement, inflammation, fever
**Explanation:** These seven named categories are explicitly listed in the Chapter 22 immunity lecture.

### mc-058 · innate
A bacterium crosses broken skin. Which action represents an immediate cellular innate defense?
**Answer:** Neutrophils and macrophages engulf microbial material
**Explanation:** Phagocytes respond without requiring prior specific antigen exposure.

### mc-059 · innate
Which immune cell is specialized to capture antigen and help activate naive T cells?
**Answer:** Dendritic cell
**Explanation:** Dendritic cells connect early antigen capture to adaptive antigen presentation.

### mc-060 · innate
Which description best fits an NK cell?
**Answer:** Innate lymphocyte able to kill some virus-infected or tumor-like abnormal cells
**Explanation:** NK cells provide cytotoxic immunological surveillance.

### mc-061 · innate
A target cell has reduced surface MHC class I expression. Which cell type may respond through missing-self recognition?
**Answer:** Natural killer cell
**Explanation:** Some NK receptors use the absence of inhibitory self-MHC signals as part of target assessment.

### mc-062 · innate
How do perforin and granzymes collaborate in cytotoxic killing?
**Answer:** Perforin helps granzyme entry, and granzymes trigger cell-death pathways
**Explanation:** NK cells and CD8 T cells use cytotoxic granule proteins to trigger apoptosis.

### mc-063 · innate
What is a major difference between NK and CD8 cytotoxic T-cell recognition?
**Answer:** NK cells use innate activating/inhibitory cues, while CD8 T cells recognize specific peptide–MHC I complexes
**Explanation:** Their killing machinery can overlap, but antigen recognition differs.

### mc-064 · innate
Which statement is most precise about the word 'nonspecific' in innate immunity?
**Answer:** Innate pathways recognize conserved threat patterns but do not use the same highly diversified receptors as adaptive lymphocytes
**Explanation:** 'Nonspecific' is the classroom category; recognition is still biologically selective.

### mc-065 · inflammation
Which classical sign of inflammation means redness?
**Answer:** Rubor
**Explanation:** Rubor is redness, commonly from increased local perfusion.

### mc-066 · inflammation
Which classical sign of inflammation means swelling?

**Explanation:** Tumor is swelling in the Latin list of inflammatory signs, not automatically a neoplasm.

### mc-067 · inflammation
Which classical sign of inflammation means heat?

**Explanation:** Calor corresponds to warmth at the inflamed site.

### mc-068 · inflammation
Which classical sign of inflammation means pain?
**Answer:** Dolor
**Explanation:** Dolor is pain associated with tissue injury and inflammatory mediators.

### mc-069 · inflammation
Why does histamine often promote visible local swelling?
**Answer:** It can increase vascular permeability so more fluid enters tissues
**Explanation:** Mast-cell mediators alter microvascular permeability and perfusion.

### mc-070 · inflammation
How do neutrophils reach inflamed tissues from nearby blood vessels?
**Answer:** Through regulated adhesion, extravasation, and chemotactic migration
**Explanation:** Leukocytes leave blood vessels and follow inflammatory chemical gradients.

### mc-071 · inflammation
What is a pyrogen's relationship to fever?
**Answer:** It helps raise the regulated temperature set point through inflammatory signaling
**Explanation:** Pyrogenic signaling involves the hypothalamus and prostaglandin pathways.

### mc-072 · inflammation
Why can a person feel cold and shiver while developing a fever?
**Answer:** The body's thermoregulatory set point has risen above current temperature
**Explanation:** Raising the set point can trigger heat-conserving and heat-generating responses.

### mc-073 · complement
Which complement fragment is a major opsonin?
**Answer:** C3b
**Explanation:** C3b coats targets, improving phagocytic uptake.

### mc-074 · complement
What is the main function of the membrane attack complex?
**Answer:** Form pores that can damage certain target-cell membranes
**Explanation:** The MAC, assembled from terminal complement proteins, can perforate susceptible membranes.

### mc-075 · complement
Which complement route can be triggered by antibodies bound to antigen?
**Answer:** Classical pathway
**Explanation:** The classical complement pathway often follows antigen–antibody complex formation.

### mc-076 · complement
Which complement pathway responds to microbial carbohydrate signatures?
**Answer:** Lectin pathway
**Explanation:** Lectin-mediated recognition can initiate complement without preexisting specific antibody.

### mc-077 · complement
What is opsonization?
**Answer:** Coating a pathogen so phagocytes recognize and ingest it more efficiently
**Explanation:** IgG and C3b are common opsonins.

### mc-078 · complement
Which complement fragment is a potent signal for inflammatory-cell recruitment?
**Answer:** C5a
**Explanation:** C5a is a strong anaphylatoxin/chemoattractant in complement activation.

### mc-079 · complement
How do type I interferons help defend neighboring cells from viruses?
**Answer:** They induce cellular antiviral programs that can inhibit viral replication
**Explanation:** Infection-induced interferon signaling can make cells less permissive to replication.

### mc-080 · complement
Which statement correctly contrasts neutralization and opsonization?
**Answer:** Neutralization blocks harmful interactions; opsonization tags a target for engulfment
**Explanation:** Antibodies can act by different effector mechanisms, including both blocking and tagging.

### mc-081 · t-cell
Which MHC class is normally expressed by nearly all nucleated cells?
**Answer:** MHC class I
**Explanation:** Class I MHC broadly displays intracellularly derived peptides.

### mc-082 · t-cell
Which pairing is the standard exam association?
**Answer:** MHC I with CD8 cytotoxic T cells
**Explanation:** CD8 recognizes class I; CD4 helper T cells recognize class II.

### mc-083 · t-cell
Which pairing is the standard exam association for helper T cells?
**Answer:** MHC II with CD4 helper T cells
**Explanation:** CD4 helper cells respond to peptides presented on MHC II.

### mc-084 · t-cell
Which cell is normally a professional MHC II antigen-presenting cell?
**Answer:** Dendritic cell
**Explanation:** Dendritic cells, macrophages, and B cells can display antigen on MHC II.

### mc-085 · t-cell
What must a naive T cell often receive in addition to antigen recognition for appropriate activation?
**Answer:** Costimulatory signals
**Explanation:** A second activation signal helps avoid inappropriate responses.

### mc-086 · t-cell
What is the chief result of activating an appropriate CD8 cytotoxic T cell?
**Answer:** Expansion of cells able to recognize and eliminate target cells bearing its peptide–MHC I antigen
**Explanation:** Antigen-specific CTLs can kill infected or abnormal target cells and form memory.

### mc-087 · t-cell
What is a key role of CD4 helper T cells in antibody-mediated defense?
**Answer:** Provide activation signals and cytokine support to appropriate B cells
**Explanation:** Helper cells coordinate B-cell differentiation and antibody responses.

### mc-088 · t-cell
Why is it misleading to say MHC I means 'this cell is infected'?
**Answer:** Normal nucleated cells express MHC I; T cells evaluate the particular peptide displayed and activation context
**Explanation:** Class I MHC is normal self-identification and antigen presentation; recognition of disease-associated peptide matters.

### mc-089 · antibody
Which cell directly secretes most antibodies during an active humoral response?
**Answer:** Plasma cell
**Explanation:** Plasma cells are specialized descendants of activated B cells.

### mc-090 · antibody
How does a B-cell receptor initially recognize antigen compared with a T-cell receptor?
**Answer:** It can bind native antigen, whereas a conventional TCR recognizes peptide presented by MHC
**Explanation:** BCR and TCR use distinct forms of antigen recognition.

### mc-091 · antibody
Which immunoglobulin class is commonly dominant in serum and important for secondary systemic responses?
**Answer:** IgG
**Explanation:** IgG is a major circulating antibody class involved in diverse effector functions.

### mc-092 · antibody
Which immunoglobulin class is commonly secreted early in a primary humoral response?
**Answer:** IgM
**Explanation:** IgM is usually an early secreted isotype in primary immune responses.

### mc-093 · antibody
Which immunoglobulin is especially important in mucus, saliva, and tears?
**Answer:** IgA
**Explanation:** Secretory IgA helps protect mucosal surfaces.

### mc-094 · antibody
Which immunoglobulin participates prominently in immediate allergic responses?
**Answer:** IgE
**Explanation:** Antigen-specific IgE on mast cells and basophils can trigger degranulation.

### mc-095 · antibody
Which class commonly functions as an antigen receptor on mature naive B cells?
**Answer:** IgD
**Explanation:** IgD is associated with the surface of naive mature B cells, often alongside IgM.

### mc-096 · antibody
What is an epitope?
**Answer:** A particular molecular site of an antigen recognized by an antibody or receptor
**Explanation:** The term antigenic determinant refers to the recognized site, not necessarily the whole pathogen.

### mc-097 · memory
Which type of immunity develops following vaccination?
**Answer:** Artificial active immunity
**Explanation:** Vaccination elicits the recipient's own adaptive response and memory.

### mc-098 · memory
Which is an example of natural passive immunity?
**Answer:** Maternal antibody transfer to an infant
**Explanation:** Passive immunity transfers protective antibody from another individual.

### mc-099 · memory
Which is an example of artificial passive immunity?
**Answer:** Administration of preformed antibodies to provide immediate protection
**Explanation:** Passive immunization supplies antibodies rather than requiring an initial endogenous response.

### mc-100 · memory
Why is a secondary adaptive response often faster than a primary response?
**Answer:** Previously formed memory lymphocytes and long-lived antibody responses can respond rapidly
**Explanation:** Memory changes the starting state of a subsequent encounter.

### mc-101 · memory
What is measured by an antibody titer?
**Answer:** Amount or functional activity of specific antibody in a specimen, often via serial dilution
**Explanation:** Titer is an assay-dependent measurement of antibody.

### mc-102 · memory
Which statement best distinguishes innate from adaptive immunity?
**Answer:** Adaptive B/T responses are highly antigen-specific and can form memory; innate mechanisms respond rapidly via conserved pathways
**Explanation:** The difference concerns recognition, timing, and memory, not whether the two systems cooperate.

### mc-103 · memory
Why does advanced HIV infection weaken many immune responses?
**Answer:** Loss/dysfunction of CD4 helper T cells disrupts coordination of adaptive immunity
**Explanation:** HIV targets CD4 T cells and causes progressive immune impairment without effective treatment.

### mc-104 · memory
What is autoimmunity?
**Answer:** Immune responses damaging the body's own tissues
**Explanation:** Autoimmune disorders result from failures in immune tolerance.
