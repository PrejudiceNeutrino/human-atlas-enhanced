# Respiratory System — Chapter 23 curriculum and content blueprint

**Stage 1 only.** This is the coverage and authoring plan, not the written articles. No educational JSON articles or study questions are claimed as completed.

## Sources and curriculum priorities

- `23-Study Guide.docx`: 29 numbered/paragraph questions, plus an 11-term terminology checklist. Guide warns the exam may cover material outside it.
- `23-Respiratory System.pptx`: 42 physiology slides spanning seven functions, airway anatomy, gas exchange, ventilation mechanics, transport, brainstem control, and pathologies.
- `Respiratory - Copy.pptx`: 19 slides emphasizing physical anatomy and classroom model identification.

The lecture’s **seven respiratory functions** are gas exchange, moving air, protection from dehydration and environmental changes, defense from pathogens/pollutants, sound production, smell, and pH balance. These must be reproduced in instructor order in General Overview and Exam Cram.

## Reading approach — match Digestive

The `General Overview` is genuinely introductory; `Deep Dive` retains anatomical and chemical explanations in detail; `How It Connects` is one continuous first-principles narrative; `Who Cares?` contains three high-impact hooks with expandable mechanism-rich explanations; and `Exam Cram` is the instructor-aligned list and table reference. ## Who Cares?

**Expected sections:** 3. **Target length:** Three compelling hooks (60–110 words each) with a roughly 350–550-word expandable “More Info” explanation each..

**Style:** Digestive/Lymphoid-style surprising title, concrete hook, precise physiological mechanism, strong distinctions and limitations; no sensational health claims.

### 1. Your lungs need a molecular anti-collapse coating every time you breathe.

**Hook:** Each alveolus is lined with a thin liquid layer whose surface tension tends to pull the air space inward. Type II pneumocytes release surfactant, reducing that tendency and making inflation far easier. It is why a liquid-lined microscopic sac can reliably reopen breath after breath.

**Expanded More Info should explain:** Air–liquid interface and surface tension; Pulmonary surfactant as lipid/protein mixture (primarily phospholipids); Type II pneumocytes, mechanics of compliance, lower work of breathing; Premature neonatal respiratory distress as a carefully labeled scientific extension.

**Lecture anchors:** physiology slides 16, 17, 18, 19 · anatomy slides 15

### 2. Your brain often cares more about carbon dioxide than oxygen when deciding to breathe.

**Hook:** At ordinary sea-level conditions, much of the moment-to-moment adjustment of breathing is driven by CO2-linked changes in acidity, not by waiting for oxygen to become dangerously low. The brain monitors the chemistry of the blood and cerebrospinal fluid to adjust how quickly CO2 leaves the lungs.

**Expanded More Info should explain:** Central chemoreceptors and cerebrospinal-fluid pH; CO2→carbonic acid→H+ and bicarbonate; Medullary/pontine regulation and ventilation feedback; Peripheral chemoreceptor response to severe hypoxemia and its limits.

**Lecture anchors:** physiology slides 36, 38, 39, 40, 41 · anatomy slides none

### 3. Your blood can release more oxygen precisely where tissue chemistry changes.

**Hook:** Hemoglobin does not merely carry oxygen like cargo in a sealed container. Its tendency to hold oxygen changes with surrounding conditions. In actively metabolizing tissues, rising CO2 and acidity can favor oxygen release—a chemical relationship often called the Bohr effect.

**Expanded More Info should explain:** Cooperative oxygen binding and the saturation curve; Higher pO2 promotes loading in lungs; Local H+/CO2 changes favor unloading, tissue context; A conceptual comparison with a dynamically adjusted delivery system, carefully avoiding intentional anthropomorphism.

**Lecture anchors:** physiology slides 34, 35, 37 · anatomy slides none

## Scientific / instructor review queue

### R01 — CO2 is not carried predominantly as carbonic acid

**Issue:** Slide says “Carbonic Acid (H2CO3) – 70%”, but most CO2 is transported as bicarbonate (HCO3−) formed after H2CO3 dissociates. Preserve the lecturer’s ~70/23/7 proportions as approximate but label the chemistry correctly.

**Proposed handling:** Explain carbonic anhydrase catalysis, H2CO3 as transient intermediate, HCO3− in plasma and chloride shift. Do not teach “70% carbonic acid” as literal final form.

### R02 — Carbon-dioxide-rich blood is not blue

**Issue:** Slide calls CO2-bound blood blue; actual venous blood remains red (usually darker). Blue in diagrams is a convention.

**Proposed handling:** Retain oxyhemoglobin/carbaminohemoglobin vocabulary, but explicitly correct blue-blood misconception.

### R03 — The term external respiration is defined broadly in the lecture

**Issue:** Lecture labels ventilation, pulmonary gas exchange and transport together as “external respiration”, while many texts reserve external respiration for pulmonary capillary gas exchange.

**Proposed handling:** Teach the three steps exactly as a course framework, then state textbook usage differs.

### R04 — Nasal nitric oxide and breathing benefits may be overstated

**Issue:** Slides attribute better diffusion and oxygen uptake to nasal breathing/NO without conditions or quantitative support; not all are established as simple universal benefits.

**Proposed handling:** Lead with grounded warming/humidification/filtration/olfaction; describe NO as possible physiological influence, not a guaranteed major boost to oxygen uptake.

### R05 — Airway histology varies within broad regions

**Issue:** The whole pharynx is not uniformly stratified squamous; nasopharynx commonly has respiratory mucosa, and nasal vestibule has squamous epithelium. Bronchioles transition in lining.

**Proposed handling:** Keep lecture shortcut for memorization with a short anatomical exception note.

### R06 — Epiglottis does not provide the only airway protection

**Issue:** Swallowing-related laryngeal elevation, aryepiglottic structures and true/false fold closure all participate; epiglottis is not a lone mechanical door.

**Proposed handling:** Explain multiple coordinated protections without belaboring it in Exam Cram.

### R07 — A rise or fall of one mmHg is an illustrative example

**Issue:** Atmospheric 760 and alveolar 759/761 mmHg depict an idealized near-sea-level situation, not fixed universal respiratory pressures.

**Proposed handling:** State the pressure difference and direction matter; absolute atmospheric pressure varies with elevation.

### R08 — Boyle law needs constant temperature and amount of gas assumptions

**Issue:** Slide presents P=1/V without proportionality constant or assumptions.

**Proposed handling:** Use P∝1/V, or P1V1=P2V2 at constant gas amount and temperature; distinguish the lung’s open airflow from simplified closed gas model.

### R09 — Legacy pontine respiratory-center labels

**Issue:** Apneustic/pneumotaxic centers are useful exam labels but modern control circuitry is distributed; pre-Bötzinger and pontine respiratory groups matter.

**Proposed handling:** Preserve DRG/VRG, apneustic, pneumotaxic exam associations; label modern framework as further scientific context.

### R10 — Quiet and forced breathing classifications are simplified

**Issue:** Slides equate forced breathing with hyperpnea and VRG as active only in forced breathing. Hyperpnea is increased ventilation, which is not exactly the same as active expiration; VRG includes rhythm-generating networks.

**Proposed handling:** Follow course language for exam while clarifying broader physiology in Deep Dive.

### R11 — Bronchoconstriction/bronchodilation is more complex than a single nerve pathway

**Issue:** Simplified sympathetic-versus-parasympathetic language is valid instructional shorthand; bronchial smooth muscle also responds to circulating epinephrine, local mediators and inflammation.

**Proposed handling:** Retain receptor-level autonomic overview, clearly mark immune/local mediators as extension.

### R12 — Residual volume and direct spirometric measurement

**Issue:** RV remains after maximal expiration and ordinary spirometry cannot directly obtain it; VC and TLC should be expressed as combinations of basic volumes.

**Proposed handling:** Make a concise table and equation set in the Deep Dive; course slides alone do not teach measurement methods in detail.

### R13 — Hemoglobin and carbon-dioxide binding terminology

**Issue:** Carbaminohemoglobin describes CO2 binding to hemoglobin, but not the primary transport form of CO2. Oxygen unloads in tissues in response to pO2 and also pH/CO2-dependent affinity.

**Proposed handling:** Avoid implying hemoglobin binds O2 and CO2 competitively at exactly the same group; include Bohr-effect caveat.

### R14 — Anoxia terminology and disease examples

**Issue:** Slide uses “apoxia”; “anoxia” is more commonly used for severe/absent oxygen supply. Instructor may expect slide terminology.

**Proposed handling:** Present the original term as used in lecture, note conventional anoxia terminology without silently changing source.

### R15 — Nasal vs lung exposure benefits and respiratory portion boundary

**Issue:** Slides define respiratory portion as “smallest bronchioles & alveoli,” but the shift begins at respiratory bronchioles; terminal bronchioles conduct air.

**Proposed handling:** Distinguish terminal bronchioles (conducting) from respiratory bronchioles (some exchange) and alveolar ducts/sacs.
